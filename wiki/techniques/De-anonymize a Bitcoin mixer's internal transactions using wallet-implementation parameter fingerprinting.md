# De-anonymize a Bitcoin mixer's internal transactions using wallet-implementation parameter fingerprinting

## Summary

Centralized mixing services obfuscate the flow of funds by pooling and reshuffling customer deposits through a chain of internal transactions before paying out to withdrawal addresses, and often further obscure this by creating "peel chains" -- long sequences of transactions that gradually peel off small amounts, imitating ordinary user spending patterns rather than an obviously mixer-like structure. While a mixer aims to make its own internal transactions statistically indistinguishable from typical user activity in terms of amounts and superficial shape, its underlying wallet software implementation still tends to construct transactions using specific, often-consistent low-level parameter choices, which -- unlike commonly-used clustering heuristics such as address-type matching, value rounding, or co-spend clustering -- are largely overlooked by both mixers seeking to hide their operation and by prior de-obfuscation research, making them a comparatively untapped signal for identifying which transactions in an ambiguous chain actually originated from the mixer.

## Details

A wallet fingerprint is built by examining a small set of transactions known with confidence to have been created by the target mixer (e.g. non-CoinJoin withdrawal and deposit-address transactions directly observed during controlled test deposits/withdrawals with the service) and recording the values and combinations of transaction-construction parameters across them: general parameters such as nVersion, transaction shape (simple spend, move, consolidation), and whether Replace-By-Fee (RBF) signaling is used; nLockTime handling patterns (whether it encodes a block height, a Unix timestamp, or is left at its default/none value, and in what combination with the nSequence field); input/output ordering conventions (whether inputs/outputs are ordered by value, lexically, or by another deterministic scheme, and whether a transaction's change output is placed at a consistent index); and address-type selection (which address types, e.g. P2WPKH, P2SH-P2WPKH, or P2PKH, the wallet uses and in what combination) plus whether "low-r-grinding" (a technique some wallets use to shorten ECDSA signatures for space efficiency) is applied. Because certain specific combinations of these parameters (e.g. a particular nVersion/nSequence/nLockTime combination) are statistically rare across the broader Bitcoin network as a whole, a transaction matching the fingerprint's characteristic combination is comparatively unlikely to have been produced by an unrelated wallet by coincidence, making fingerprint matching a useful complementary signal for traversing an otherwise ambiguous peel chain -- particularly at the specific decision points (which output of a <1:2> transaction is the mixer-controlled change output, and which is the value being paid out) where conventional heuristics like address-type matching and value rounding do not reliably resolve the ambiguity.

## Examples

- Applied to a real mixing service (Anonymixer), 23 controlled deposit and 20 controlled withdrawal sessions were used to construct the wallet fingerprint, revealing that the service's implementation randomizes nVersion, RBF signaling, and nLockTime handling in seemingly independent or inconsistent-timing combinations that occur rarely in the broader network -- for example, the specific combination of nVersion=2 with an explicitly-disabled locktime and an assigned block-height nLockTime value was found in only about 1.3% of transactions in a roughly 1,000-block network-wide sample, letting its presence serve as comparatively strong corroborating evidence for mixer involvement when it appears in an otherwise-ambiguous transaction.
- Traversing forward and backward from a small set of seed addresses using the constructed fingerprint successfully followed multiple peel chains through a total of 0.8534 BTC in identified volume across 344 identified service addresses and 461 identified customer addresses, and independently confirmed several resulting customer withdrawal addresses via open-source intelligence as being associated with other known services (exchanges) or with reports of scam/ransomware activity on community websites.
- The fingerprint was used to correctly resolve an ambiguous change-address decision within a peel chain that conventional clustering heuristics could not reliably determine, by identifying which of two candidate output addresses in a <1:2> transaction matched the mixer's known parameter-combination fingerprint rather than the customer's typical wallet fingerprint.
